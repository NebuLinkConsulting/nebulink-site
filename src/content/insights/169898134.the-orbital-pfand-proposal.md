# **The structural habit of doing the right thing**

As anyone who has met me knows, I am always travelling somewhere. However, there’s one place I’ve consistently returned to. Hamburg, a bustling port city in northern Germany which has many parallels with my home city of Manchester. From the red brick buildings by the canals, to the underground music scene, and even to the uprising of overpriced, venture capital backed coffee shops.

Despite the vast array of similarities, there is one major difference that always sticks with me whenever I return to the UK. The idea of civic responsibility that sits deep within the German mindset. It is the small things that impress me, where Germans regularly take actions for the greater good of society. This includes things like the infamous quiet hours, where you cannot so much as turn on a washing machine too late at night without risking disturbing the peace. This stretches further: within the first few weeks of living in Germany several years ago I was publicly and sharply criticised by strangers, often grandmas, for crossing the road before the light went green, and even now I am impressed by the fact that German parks often have dedicated BBQ sections which are filled with hundreds of people, including immigrants and other foreigners, who are meticulously following the rules.

This civic responsibility isn’t innate to Germans; it is engineered by public policy. As the title suggests, the part that impresses me most is the Pfand system: a deposit return scheme where every drink container carries a small, refundable stake. You pay it at purchase and get it back when you return the empty bottle or can, drop it in a reverse vending machine or hand it to the shop, and the system does the rest. The deposit is large enough that people care, small enough not to feel punitive, and the logistics are frictionless, so compliance becomes the default; return rates sit around 98 percent.

Crucially, it internalises the cost of waste without preaching: if you don’t return the container, you lose the deposit, which in aggregate funds the system’s operation and creates a buffer for sustainability, while responsible behaviour is rewarded immediately. That is the logic I want to transplant to orbit, a refundable “orbital Pfand” posted up front, returned only after verified end of life disposal or safe deorbiting, with forfeited stakes underwriting cleanup or compensating future users.

# **Why Sustainability Fails Today**

Yes, I can hear the obvious objection, I run a space consulting firm, why invoke German civic society? Because the mechanism that makes compliance habitual in Hamburg is the same structural lever missing in orbital governance. As the NewSpace industry is rapidly maturing, I believe that the system should be built in a responsible way, one that looks after the shared commons of low Earth orbit. At the moment, there are countless efforts led by well-meaning groups to improve space sustainability. Almost every major space agency trots out sustainability as a headline, with new research being funded every year. A specific example of this is the European Space Agency’s Zero Debris Charter, a non-legally binding initiative that is fostering a community of proactive actors working collectively towards jointly defined, measurable targets for 2030.

The language can feel dense and bureaucratic, but there are specific and quantitative goals inside, including avoiding intentional debris release and keeping the probability of space debris generation below one in a thousand over an object’s lifetime. Fine, on paper. But this, alongside almost all other standards, are voluntary agreements with no binding teeth. We are currently relying on commercial actors whose fiduciary obligations push them to maximise shareholder returns to voluntarily constrain behaviour that degrades a shared commons over time.

Once again, as I always say, this is not a new problem unique to the space industry. It is a concept as old as classical economic thought, more commonly known as the tragedy of the commons. This is where, if many people enjoy unrestricted access to a finite, valuable resource, most typically referring to a pasture, they will tend to overuse it and it often ends up with the destruction of the shared good. The term was popularised by Garrett Hardin in his 1968 article titled “The Tragedy of the Commons,” although its roots trace back to William Forster Lloyd’s earlier pamphlet, Two Lectures on the Checks to Population, which used the example of cattle herders sharing common grazing land. Lloyd noted that the individually rational decision would be for each farmer to add more cattle, but if every farmer did that, the pasture would be depleted.

Therein is the tragedy.

“Each man is locked into a system that compels him to increase his herd without limit, in a world that is limited. Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush, each pursuing his own best interest in a society that believes in the freedom of the commons. Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all.”  
– Garrett Hardin, _The Tragedy of the Commons_, _Science_, 1968

# **The Structural Fault Lines in the Space Economy**

This is especially problematic given the economic system we find ourselves in. The vast majority of satellites today are operated by companies, either publicly traded or, more commonly, backed by private investors who are, rightly, seeking to maximise the return on their investments. Every modern company is racing to launch more satellites, serve more customers, and increase the return to shareholders. The misalignment in the space industry is amplified by four structural factors:

*   Humans are poor at accurately estimating risks, they systematically underestimate tail risk because conventional risk models assume thin tails and rely on misleading averages. Catastrophes like the 2008 financial crisis, COVID-19 and climate change exposed these failures. Even professional risk managers mispriced the likelihood and consequences. Taleb’s _[Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails](https://codowd.com/bigdata/misc/Taleb_Statistical_Consequences_of_Fat_Tails.pdf)_ is a rigorous, if lengthy, treatment of this.
    
*   The actors creating space debris are not the ones who will bear its full cost. Immediate economic gains from launching and operating additional satellites accrue privately, while the cumulative degradation of the orbital environment, higher collision probabilities, reduced access, cascade risks, is felt collectively by future users. That creates a classic negative externality with perverse incentive alignment.
    
*   Commercial actors are structurally short termist. Investors and markets reward rapid deployment and revenue growth, so companies prioritise near term returns over preserving orbital sustainability. You might think you are an ethical space engineer, but there is a strong chance your space startup will be acquired by private equity firms and its original values diluted or discarded.
    
*   No one owns orbit, so individual choices add up to collective harm. Each operator acting in their own interest increases traffic and systemic risk, collisions generate debris, which further destabilises the environment in feedback loops. There is no global enforcement authority, no space environmental protection agency, no effective adjudication mechanism.
    

# **For a sustainable future in space**

Space sustainability isn’t a technical problem waiting for a better model, it is an incentive problem. The Pfand shows the fix: require operators to post a refundable, risk calibrated stake, return it only after independently verified end of life behaviour, and use forfeited deposits to fund cleanup or compensate future users. Embed that mechanism in licences, financing covenants and procurement. Launch pilots with a coalition of willing states and commercial partners, and publish performance publicly. Give actors skin in the game, and the race to overload orbit becomes a mechanism for its stewardship, fail to do so and humanity may be stuck on Earth looking up at an impassable field of debris caused by our actions today.
