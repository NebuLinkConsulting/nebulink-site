# Why the ISS Retirement Changes Everything

The retirement of the ISS marks the end of the era of government-owned and operated human spaceflight infrastructure in LEO. This transition is not driven by technical or commercial demand, but by policy and procurement priorities. Governments no longer wish to carry the long-term operational burden of orbital platforms.

In response, a wave of commercial space station ventures has emerged, often with government backing. However, these projects risk reproducing the same structural issues that challenged the ISS: high costs, bespoke infrastructure, limited accessibility, and ongoing dependence on public subsidy.

This paper evaluates the foundations of the ISS model and proposes alternative approaches informed by adjacent sectors. It argues that future space stations must function either as modular service platforms or as equity-sharing ventures embedded in the success of onboard activities. These models, coupled with transitional public funding and global buy-in, offer a scalable path toward sustainable orbital infrastructure.

# Why the ISS Model is Flawed

## A broken funding formala

While the ISS has delivered scientific, technical, and diplomatic value, its cost structure is fundamentally unsustainable. NASA recovers <5% of operating costs from commercial users. The majority of funding comes from US taxpayers, with modest contributions from international partners. As public budgets tighten and political focus shifts, this model is no longer viable. Agencies now look to commercial industry, not just to supplement ISS capabilities, but to replace them entirely, without a proven economic blueprint for doing so.

## Bespoke, one of a kind, and costly

Despite lower launch costs, orbital infrastructure remains prohibitively expensive due to its design philosophy. Current stations are still bespoke, human-rated systems, each with unique hardware, integration requirements, and operational constraints. This makes them slow to build, costly to maintain, and difficult to scale. The contrast within the space sector is instructive: standardised buses, COTS components, and batch production are dramatically cutting costs elsewhere. Until orbital infrastructure is treated as a product, not a prototype, commercialisation is out of reach.

## Space tourism won’t pay the bills

Space tourism is touted as a solution, but it offers limited economic value. The addressable market is small, highly price-sensitive, and not designed for repeat business, often structured as prestige experiences, one-time ticket sales to a narrow group of individuals, rather than recurring infrastructure use.

## The limits of research revenue

Research holds value, but it cannot sustain a space station. Most research demand is publicly funded, one-off, and has limited opportunity for repeat business. This model, selling access, offers insufficient volume to support permanent infrastructure. Without an industrial base, research cannot provide the revenue needed to replace government subsidies.

# Does this problem exist in other industries?

Despite what everyone tells you, space isn’t that special and unique. For example, both biotechnology and advanced manufacturing, providers confront the same barriers that face commercial space stations: massive upfront capital requirements, specialised hardware integration and a fragmented customer base of small, under-funded innovators.

Yet these sectors have overcome those hurdles by productising access and sharing risk and reward with their users, but how?

In **biotech** incubators such as Johnson & Johnson’s JLABS or Ginkgo Bioworks’ Foundry, infrastructure providers do far more than lease space. They offer bundled services including laboratory equipment, regulatory guidance and business development support, often reducing or waiving bench fees in exchange for a 5 to 10% equity stake or a royalty on future product sales. This model aligns incentives by sharing downside risk with cash-strapped startups and participating directly in upside when a therapy wins FDA approval or a synthetic biology product enters commercial production.

Similarly, advanced manufacturing ecosystems such as Protolabs’ on-demand CNC and 3D printing services and the Fraunhofer Institutes’ pilot production lines combine standardised tooling, rapid prototyping and quality certification into a manufacturing-as-a-service offering. Clients pay predictable per-part fees rather than investing capital in bespoke machinery, while providers amortise their platforms across multiple users. In both industries, productised access, transparent pricing and shared-risk investment lower entry barriers, accelerate time to market and transform fixed-cost facilities into scalable, value-generating platforms. Commercial space stations must adopt the same playbook.

# Two business models for sustainable space stations

Now that we know how other industries have solved it, let’s translate this into specific models for space.

## Platform-as-a-Service for Orbit

To eliminate access friction and turn microgravity into a repeatable service, a commercial station must be productised end-to-end. Rather than bespoke, one-off missions, operators need to offer:

1.  **Standardised Modules** that any payload can plug into without custom integration
    
2.  **Transparent, Usage-Based Pricing** per kilogram, per week or per experiment type
    
3.  **Pre-Defined Integration Pathways** with set SLAs for payload delivery, launch windows and on-station support
    

This could be used in pharmaceutical R&D, advanced materials testing and in-orbit manufacturing trials. By treating microgravity like a utility, customers gain predictable turnaround times and costs, whilst operators benefit from higher capacity utilisation and lower per-mission overhead.

## Equity-Sharing: Betting on Onboard Success

Even a ‘space-compute’ model needs working capital. Borrowing biotech incubator mechanics, stations should offer reduced or waived access fees in exchange for:

*   **Royalties** on revenue generated by onboard products (for example, a share of drug sales)
    
*   **Equity Stakes** in startups that commercialise novel materials or technologies developed in microgravity
    

This approach turns an infrastructure cost center into an appreciating asset. Startups avoid prohibitive upfront fees, while station operators participate directly in the upside of every successful payload, aligning incentives for long-term collaboration and platform improvement.

# Building the Broader Orbital Ecosystem

With our two core business models in place, we must now cultivate the market and policy landscape that will sustain them. Commercial stations cannot succeed in isolation; they require a healthy, interdependent ecosystem.

## Creating Market Pull through Infrastructure

Station operators should become proactive market builders.

Emerging sectors such as in-space servicing, assembly and manufacturing and orbital logistics demand demonstration environments in orbit. By establishing dedicated robotics corridors, precision docking modules and standardised payload interfaces, stations can host regular technology validation campaigns in partnership with satellite operators and service providers. These testbeds reduce development risk for innovators and provide predictable cadence for station utilisation.

Long lead times and bespoke integration currently deter early adopters; a conveyor-belt approach to experiment integration, coupled with guaranteed uptime and on-station support services, will dramatically shorten sales cycles and lock in recurring revenues.

Beyond hardware, operators can curate thematic calls for proposals in areas such as refuelling robotics, modular habitat assembly and microgravity electronics fabrication. These campaigns, financed through modest user fees and sponsored by public-private consortia, will further stimulate demand and position the station as the proving ground of choice for next-generation orbital technologies.

## Global Co-Investment: From Users to Stakeholders

The next generation of stations must transform occasional customers into invested allies.

Rather than simply selling access, operators can structure co-investment vehicles that grant participating nations, agencies and commercial users an ownership stake in the station’s future. For example, a consortium of emerging spacefaring countries could contribute capital in return for equity shares proportionate to their investment. Similarly, anchor commercial customers, such as pharmaceutical companies or materials producers, could secure preferred rates in exchange for a minority equity stake or a capped royalty on their onboard revenues.

This model aligns long-term incentives and creates a stable capital base. Governance can be formalised through a station council composed of equity partners, ensuring strategic direction reflects stakeholder interests. By broadening the ownership roster beyond traditional partners, stations will benefit from diversified funding sources, enhanced diplomatic support and a ready-made customer pipeline. Passive users become part of the station’s board, deepening their commitment and reducing the risk of abrupt funding withdrawals.

Next-generation stations offer an opportunity to rebalance this model. India, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, and the UAE are expanding their space sectors and have strategic, technical, and diplomatic incentives to participate. Co-investment, shared equity, and hosted national missions can turn passive interest into active partnership, broadening the financial base and enhancing geopolitical stability.

## Phasing Out Subsidies, Spurring Commercial Rev-Ops

Despite these new models, government funding remains essential for the near future.

Public funding remains essential during the station’s infancy, but it must be delivered in a way that encourages market maturity. A tiered subsidy framework offers a clear runway for commercial viability. In the first phase, governments cover 100 percent of pre-commercial research and development activities up to Technology Readiness Level 3, de-risking foundational experiments.

In the second phase, agencies transition to cost-sharing, funding 50 percent of integration and demonstration projects between TRL 4 and TRL 6. This model ensures that novel applications such as microgravity drug synthesis or precision manufacturing receive focused support without encouraging perpetual subsidy dependence. By TRL 7 and above, all operating costs shift to commercial users, who by then have validated revenue streams and repeat business models.

To facilitate this transition, agencies can offer milestone-based bridge contracts or revenue guarantees that taper as private orders increase. Clear sunset dates for each funding phase create urgency for commercial customers to prove their business cases.

This graduated approach not only incentivises private investment but also provides a predictable timeline for governments to exit operational funding, ensuring a sustainable handover from public subsidy to market-driven growth.

# A Roadmap Beyond the ISS: Your Next Steps

The end of the ISS era is not just a handover to the private sector. It is a chance to redefine what a space station is for. Without new thinking, commercial successors will inherit the same economic fragility.

Instead, we must adopt the platform-as-a-service and equity-sharing frameworks outlined above to create stations that generate value, attract users and compound returns over time.

To move from theory to reality, organisations should:

1.  **Pilot Modular Service Offerings**: Develop a small-scale demonstration of standardised modules and transparent pricing for microgravity experiments, using existing commercial vehicles or hosted payloads.
    
2.  **Structure Equity Partnerships**: Convene potential station customers, governments, commercial R&D firms and emerging space nations, to explore joint-investment agreements and royalty models that align long-term incentives.
    
3.  **Engage Policymakers**: Advocate for a tiered subsidy framework that supports low-TRL research, phases out operational grants by TRL 7 and introduces milestone-based bridge contracts.
    

By taking these steps now, stakeholders can ensure the next generation of space stations not only sustains itself but also expands what is possible in orbit, enabling new industries, partnerships and scientific breakthroughs.

But it must do more than sustain itself. It must create the conditions for what comes next. Commercial stations should not follow the ISS, they should make it possible to leave it behind.
