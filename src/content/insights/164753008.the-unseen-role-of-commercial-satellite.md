Sub-metre satellite imagery was once the exclusive domain of government agencies. This premise has been upended by massive industry shifts where today, anyone with a credit card can task a satellite. This has begun to revolutionise industries across the world, from corporate intelligence to disaster response, however, this revolution also arms a far more sinister clientele: terrorists and militaries

From Kashmir’s Pahalgam through to Khartoum’s neighbourhoods, todays commercial space operators have become unwilling participants in terror attacks and troop movements. This article today explores the use cases.

# Case Studies

## **1\. Russian Missile Strikes in Ukraine (February–December 2022)**

Between February and December 2022, The [Atlantic’s Graeme Wood](https://www.theatlantic.com/international/archive/2024/03/american-satellites-russia-ukraine-war/677775/) identified a striking pattern: more than 350 instances in which commercial satellite images of Ukrainian sites were purchased just days before those same locations were hit by Russian missile strikes. Journalists noted that these taskings spanned deep into Ukrainian territory, far beyond frontline areas, and raised the question of whether ostensibly civilian imagery feeds were being repurposed for targeting.

[Molfar analysis](https://molfar.com/en/blog/chy-kupuyut-rosiyany-v-maxar-i-planet-foto-shchob-obstrilyuvaty-ukrainu?utm_source=chatgpt.com) set out to verify this claim by examining 321 confirmed Russian strikes from 24 February to 31 December 2022. They found that in 277 cases, over 85 percent, the exact target area had indeed been imaged one to two days before impact, covering bridges, ammunition depots and defensive positions around Kharkiv and Mykolaiv. By routing orders through intermediary brokers and shell companies, planners bypassed export controls and obtained sub-metre data for under USD 500 per scene, materially sharpening strike precision.

To test how easily these taskings could slip through vetting, Molfar’s HUMINT team posed as foreign buyers and approached several Maxar and Planet resellers. Despite sanctions and corporate policies prohibiting sales to sanctioned actors, some distributors offered imagery with minimal identity checks, demonstrating how intermediaries can enable access to sensitive data even when primary providers deny direct sales. This finding lays bare the ethical blind spot in today’s open satellite marketplaces and underscores the urgent need for tighter end-use controls.

## **2\. Houthi Attacks on Red Sea Shipping (October 2024)**

In April 2025, the [Financial Times reported that Chang Guang Satellite Technology Co. Ltd, a commercial Chinese satellite firm with deep ties to the People’s Liberation Army, was supplying near-real-time imagery to Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen](https://www.ft.com/content/628b404b-2a24-4853-8f3c-9caad408ef8f). According to U.S. officials cited by the FT, Chang Guang’s commercial division routed sub-metre optical and radar data through Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, enabling the Houthis to monitor U.S. and allied naval movements in the southern Red Sea with unprecedented clarity.

A [Wall Street Journal investigation](https://www.wsj.com/world/china/the-chinese-satellite-firm-washington-accuses-of-helping-u-s-foes-b5e68d2e) added that these brokered feeds were augmented by commercial vessel-tracking services, which together improved the rebels’ hit probability by over 30 percent compared to unguided attacks. The WSJ detailed how Houthi planners used precise geospatial updates, sourced via Russian intermediaries and Chang Guang’s imagery, to synchronise missile and drone launches against both merchant ships and naval vessels, turning ostensibly civilian data into battlefield-grade targeting intelligence.

In response to rising concerns, the [European Commission and ESA have begun deliberately degrading or delaying public Sentinel-2 coverage over the Red Sea corridor](https://hntrbrk.com/eu-satellites/). Sentinel-2’s high-resolution, multispectral imagery is temporarily withheld for 24–48-hour periods in critical zones, a move officials say aligns with “applicable regulations and operational considerations” and aims to deny hostile actors unfettered access to real-time monitoring feeds. This new restriction marks one of the first instances in which an open-data programme has been selectively throttled to protect maritime traffic from precision-guided threats.

## **3\. Pahalgam Ambush in Jammu & Kashmir, (April 2025)**

Between 2 February and 12 April, [tasking requests for sub-metre imagery of Pahalgam more than doubled on Maxar’s portal](https://theprint.in/defence/pahalgam-satellite-image-us-space-tech-firm-maxar-technologies/2620666/), with a dozen separate orders zeroing in on the town’s roads, hotels and valley overlooks. The final request arrived on 12 April, ten days before terrorists opened fire on a tourist convoy in Baisaran Valley, killing twenty-six civilians.

Early reports linked the surge in orders to Maxar’s former partner, Pakistan-linked Business Systems International. Within hours of that claim, Maxar removed BSI from its list of resellers and issued a categorical denial. An [India Today TV report](https://www.indiatoday.in/india/story/maxar-satellite-experts-imagery-pahalgam-terror-attack-india-pak-conflict-terrorists-2723622-2025-05-12) quoted a Maxar spokesperson: “Our records indicate that BSI has neither placed any tasking orders over Pahalgam or the surrounding areas this year, nor ordered any imagery through our archive.” Intelligence analysts echoed that local sleeper-cell support and readily available public maps made high-end taskings unnecessary for an ambush carried out at point-blank range.

Geospatial experts, including ASPI’s Nathan Ruser, found no abnormal imaging patterns. Ruser noted that on-demand imagery markets have expanded to include dozens of global providers and mobile-app ordering, so spikes alone do not prove malicious intent. Still, the timing and focus of these twelve taskings remain striking. Whether driven by curiosity, research or reconnaissance, even if they didn’t supply those images, the fact that taskings occurred so shortly before the attack underscores the ethical blind spot in today’s open satellite marketplaces.

## **4\. National governments are worried about easing restrictions (2001, 2020)**

Governments maintain ultimate control over high-resolution commercial imagery through a mix of licensing, resolution caps and “shutter control” provisions that date back decades. In the United States, the Land Remote Sensing Policy Act of 1992 requires NOAA licenses for any domestic satellite operator, and grants the executive branch authority to deny or delay image collection over sensitive areas. Rather than invoke formal shutter closures, Washington has typically employed what critics call “checkbook shutter control”—purchasing exclusive rights to imagery instead. After 9/11, for example, the [Pentagon bought sole access to all Ikonos satellite pictures of Afghanistan from Space Imaging](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2001/oct/17/physicalsciences.afghanistan), blocking civilian and media outlets from viewing detailed post-strike scenes without any public legal order to do so .

In July 2020, [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/israel-feels-exposed-as-us-drops-satellite-imaging-cap-idUSKBN2470VD/) reported that the US eased restrictions under the Kyl–Bingaman Amendment to allow American satellite firms to sell imagery of Israel and the Palestinian territories at resolutions up to 0.4 m, down from the previous 2 m limit. A senior Israeli Defence Ministry official warned that such high-resolution data could be exploited by terrorist groups like Hezbollah and Hamas to pinpoint both military installations and civilian infrastructure for rocket attacks and sabotage. Despite assurances that foreign providers already offer sub-2 m imagery, Israel has urged renewed scrutiny of end-use policies to prevent its enemies from gaining a precision-strike advantage.

# Policies

## **Why Existing Safeguards Have Fallen Short**

These cases share three systemic gaps:

1.  **Intermediary Opacity**  
    Shell companies and geospatial brokers obscure true end users, leaving providers unaware of ultimate intent.
    
2.  **Marketplace Blindspots**  
    On-demand portals lack real-time anomaly detection. Repeated taskings over sensitive sites sail through unflagged.
    
3.  **Regulatory Loopholes**  
    Export controls focus on satellite hardware transfers, not downstream data distribution once imagery is in orbit.
    

The result is a buyer’s market for targeting intelligence: minimal marginal cost per image, yet decisive tactical gains.

## **Existing Safeguards**

Before we can close the ethical gap, it helps to understand the measures already in place:

1.  **Provider Vetting & End-Use Declarations**  
    Commercial imagery firms require new customers to complete Know-Your-Customer checks and sign end-use agreements attesting they will not resell data to sanctioned or prohibited actors. Many also screen orders against embargoed-area lists and maintain internal compliance teams to flag suspicious taskings.
    
2.  **Export-Control Regimes**  
    In the US, the Land Remote Sensing Policy Act (1992) mandates NOAA licences for any domestic satellite operator and empowers “shutter control” to deny or delay collection over specified sites. The Kyl–Bingaman Amendment further caps U.S. providers’ spatial resolution at 2 m (recently eased to 0.4 m) for imagery over Israel and the Palestinian territories. Similar licensing frameworks exist, albeit less uniformly in Europe and Australia.
    
3.  **Shutter & “Checkbook” Controls**  
    While formal shutter closures are rare, governments often purchase exclusive tasking rights to sensitive areas (e.g. U.S. take-rights to Ikonos imagery after 9/11) to keep high-resolution data off the open market. This “checkbook shutter” approach can be faster and less visible than regulatory bans.
    
4.  **Sanctions Screening & Reseller Oversight**  
    Major providers integrate global sanctions lists into their order‐processing pipelines and vet downstream resellers through audit provisions in their partner contracts. However, opaque broker networks and shell companies can still mask end users.
    
5.  **International Data-Sharing Agreements**  
    Some open-data programmes, like ESA’s Copernicus and NASA’s Landsat, rely on “free and open” policies but reserve the right to impose temporary coverage gaps or delays for national-security reasons, as demonstrated by the recent Sentinel-2 throttling over the Red Sea.
    

Despite these layers of oversight, the cases outlined above reveal that determined actors can still slip through, or altogether bypass, existing controls. The next section will propose targeted enhancements to shore up these weak links.

## **Closing the Gap: Strengthening Safeguards**

Even with vetting, licensing and shutter controls in place, determined actors continue to exploit intermediary channels and jurisdictional loopholes. To prevent routine commercial imagery from becoming an unwitting force multiplier, providers and regulators should consider:

1.  **Transparent Tasking Metadata Feeds**  
    Require resellers and brokers to publish anonymised logs of all tasking requests over predefined “sensitive” coordinates (hospitals, schools, critical bridges, ports). Publicly accessible metadata; time, resolution, general area, would enable OSINT communities to flag suspicious surges before strikes occur.
    
2.  **Dynamic Watchlists with Automated Triage**  
    Maintain and regularly update geo-watchlists of sites at high risk of misuse. Orders matching those lists should trigger automated risk scores, escalating borderline cases for manual review. Machine-learning models can learn from past incidents to reduce false positives while ensuring genuine alerts receive human attention.
    
3.  **Multilateral End-Use Licensing Framework**  
    Expand beyond unilateral export controls by forging a coalition of major imagery-producing nations (US, EU, China, India). A harmonised treaty, similar to the Wassenaar Arrangement, would standardise definitions of dual-use data, impose joint vetting standards and establish shared penalties for non-compliance.
    
4.  **Provider–OSINT Partnerships**  
    Formalise “red-flag” cooperation between satellite operators and reputable OSINT groups. Providers could integrate anonymised honeypot taskings into their order flows; when these decoys are requested, the system issues an internal alert, prompting compliance teams to investigate potential bad actors without compromising client confidentiality.
    
5.  **Periodic Independent Audits**  
    Mandate third-party audits of compliance programmes at both primary providers and major reseller networks. Public summaries of audit findings would build market trust and demonstrate proactive efforts to close ethical blind spots.
    

# **Conclusion**

Commercial satellite imagery underpins critical civilian and humanitarian applications, but its dual-use nature demands a recalibrated approach. By embracing transparency, automation, international cooperation and independent oversight, the industry can preserve the promise of on-demand Earth observation while denying adversaries the means to turn those same pixels into precision strikes.
