**Back to 1999: How space graduate pay has (not) changed**

Last year, at an ESA networking dinner in Spain, I spoke with a senior engineer who started his career in the British space sector in the late 1990s. His first graduate role paid over £20,000 a year, equivalent to about £45,000–£50,000 today once adjusted for inflation. Yet aerospace engineering graduates in the UK now typically start on £25,000–£35,000, with only a handful of roles breaching £40,000. In real terms, starting pay has barely budged in 25 years, despite soaring living costs, mounting student debt, and increasingly complex clearance requirements.

It is not just a nominal stagnation, but a systemic undervaluing of technical talent. While minimum wage and cost-of-living pressures nudged headline salaries above £30,000, the underlying strategy remains one of underinvestment. If space is truly a strategic sector, it deserves strategic pay.

**Harwell: A Launchpad You Can't Afford**

Even the meagre salaries above are hollow if you cannot afford to live near the job.

Take Harwell. It’s promoted as the UK’s space innovation hub. It hosts the UK Space Agency, Open Cosmos, Satellite Applications Catapult, and numerous startups. On paper, it's a success, a walkable concentration of the nation’s top talent. In reality, it’s a car-dependent science park surrounded by Oxfordshire rents, with jobs that don’t even guarantee access to parking. I ran the numbers once to move to Harwell. I would have needed a £10,000 pay rise to match my lifestyle in Manchester, a location which is also expensive but somehow cheaper than Harwell.

At the time of writing, a room in a shared house near Harwell averages £834 per month. For a graduate on a £30,000 salary, post-tax take-home pay is around £1,970 per month. Rent alone takes up over 42% of their income, before bills, groceries, or transport.

This isn’t an edge case. It’s the default. Many of these “strategic” jobs are located in places where even shared housing is unaffordable on a graduate salary.

**The Unspoken Elite Track**

As Lindsay Clark noted in her original Register article, the space sector’s competitor for talent isn’t just banking and consulting; it’s every job that offers better pay and fewer barriers. Indeed, Clark cites entry-level salaries of **£80,000–£90,000** in investment banking and management consulting as eye-watering.

In reality, MBB roles start at £50k base, bulge-bracket banking analysts at £60k base, and HFTs like Jane Street at around £150k. But for the typical STEM graduate, these roles are effectively out of reach. Securing internships in your first year, navigating unpaid or low‑paid placements, and leveraging alumni networks at prestigious institutions form a gauntlet that excludes the majority.

I worked at a leading space consultancy and was one of the few without a Russell Group pedigree or a portfolio of internships. The rest came with the right CV, connections, and clearance, resources not available to most. The question is not whether UK space pay lags banking, it’s whether it competes with realistic alternatives.

**Beyond the UK: Europe Pays More**

Talent flows to where it is paid, and Europe is taking note.

I lived in mainland Europe for several years, both before and after Brexit. The shift is obvious. Before, the UK was a top destination for space talent, English-speaking, globally connected, and relatively well-paid. That appeal has declined dramatically.

I recently spent a month in Torino, home to several Italian space firms. Over aperitifs, I spoke with engineers at both startups and major primes. None said they would consider relocating to the UK… not because of weather, culture, or visa bureaucracy, but because they’d earn the same, or more, staying in Italy. With the post-Brexit slide in the pound and high UK living costs, the economics simply don’t work. I appreciate this is anecdotal evidence, so I have also grabbed some salary datapoints from across Europe.

**Italy**: Leonardo’s graduate engineers report starting salaries of **€33,000–€35,000** (£28,000–£30,000), with clear, incremental increases around year two and three. Combined with lower rents outside Milan and strong public healthcare, the real take-home is competitive with UK figures, if not better.

**Germany**: Under IG Metall agreements, entry roles begin at **€50,000–€65,000** (£42,000–£55,000), escalating by union-negotiated cost-of-living raises. Generous sick pay, 30 days leave, and robust unemployment benefits add to the package. Here, an engineer’s wage goes beyond salary to genuine livelihood security.

**Netherlands & Sweden**: Early-career roles in ESA-affiliated sites like Noordwijk pay **€45,000–€60,000** (£38,000–£50,000), sometimes with housing allowances and subsidised transport. In Stockholm or Gothenburg, space-adjacent tech jobs in satcom and sensor fusion fetch similar figures, alongside shorter working weeks and parental support

**Introducing Dishoom, where pouring pints pays beats space engineering on pay**

If continental Europe outbids us, what about domestic competition? Shelter from austerity rarely comes from public coffers, but rather from the hospitality sector.

I gathered current data from Glassdoor, Gradcracker, and company career pages:

*   **Graduate space roles**: £28,000–£34,000
    
*   **Specialist positions**: up to £40,000
    
*   **Senior 5‑year engineers**: £35,000–£45,000
    

By contrast, at Dishoom, a well‑run, tip‑inclusive restaurant group, servers earn:

*   **£16/hr base** = £33,280/year
    
*   **£18–20/hr with tips** = £37,000–£41,000/year
    
*   **Team Management** \= up to £45,000

Space engineering is a strategically important industry, but you’d get paid the same serving curries

No degree, no clearance, no multi-stage hiring. Just consistent, fair pay. In effect, a server at Dishoom can out‑earn an engineer designing satellite subsystems, especially when you realise they don’t face student loan payments.

This isn’t a criticism of hospitality, far from it. It’s refreshing to see a company pay staff properly. The issue is the disconnect between how we talk about ‘strategic sectors’ like space, and how we actually treat the people expected to work in them. If the UK wants to build a sovereign capability, it cannot rely on goodwill and underpaid engineers.

**Space Sovereignty Starts with a Competitive Paycheck**

We champion STEM education, publish space strategy white papers, and promise sovereignty in orbit. But sovereignty requires people.

Right now, the UK space sector offers:

*   **Starting salaries** that have fallen behind inflation and continental peers
    
*   **Locations** that exclude the very graduates we need
    
*   **Progression** that lags service and software industr
    

The result is a brain drain not to Wall Street, but to hospitality, European labs, and tech hubs that value engineers properly.

Here’s the unsparing truth:

**You can earn more serving curries than building satellites.**

Until government and industry align pay with promise, the UK will continue to lose its brightest, and space will remain a distant dream rather than a national achievement.

Not to Silicon Valley. Not to Wall Street. Just to somewhere they can pay their rent.
