A quick case study that cuts launch costs in half just by swapping out obsolete space hardware. No redesigns, no hype, just updated assumptions.

## **The economics of putting a data centre in space**

I recently came across a post estimating what it would take to launch a top-end AI rack, specifically the **[NVIDIA](https://www.linkedin.com/company/nvidia/)** GB200 NVL72, into orbit. The author walked through the power draw, cooling requirements, and launch cost, concluding that it would cost around 20 million dollars just to get it up there.

At first glance, the numbers looked reasonable. But the assumptions were built around 1990s space hardware. The post used ISS solar arrays, legacy ammonia radiators, and made no attempt to update the architecture for what is available today. That got me thinking. What if we ran the same problem using current-generation systems?

This article is a case study. I keep the rack constant and only swap out the outdated power and thermal systems. Even with that limited change, the launch mass drops by half and the cost comes down by over 10 million dollars. And that is before we address the rack itself, which, as I will explain later, is completely unsuited to orbital deployment.

Let’s run the numbers.

## **This Is Not Just a Thought Experiment**

This is a problem worth solving, because this is no longer a speculative topic. China has already begun deploying its **Star Compute** constellation, integrating onboard AI models into a massive distributed space-based network. **[Axiom Space](https://www.linkedin.com/company/axiom-space/)** is preparing to deploy on-orbit data processing nodes aboard **[Kepler Communications Inc.](https://www.linkedin.com/company/kepler-communications-inc-/)**, starting with a test deployment and eventually building its own orbital data centres. **[Starcloud](https://www.linkedin.com/company/starcloudinc/)**, a Y Combinator-backed company, has raised tens of millions of dollars to build orbital data centres, with a demonstrator flight scheduled to launch this summer. New entrants like **[Sophia Space](https://www.linkedin.com/company/sophia-space/)** are appearing almost monthly.

This is not a theoretical debate about the future. It is a present-day race to operationalise orbital compute. Which is why it is important to get the numbers right.

## **Where the $20 Million Figure Came From**

The original post was a rough estimate of what it would take to launch a high-performance data centre rack into orbit. The author chose the NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, assumed a power draw of around 120 kilowatts, and worked out the mass needed for solar arrays and cooling using International Space Station hardware as a reference. Then they multiplied the total mass by Falcon 9 launch costs and arrived at a price tag of about $20 million.

At a high level, here is what the post assumed:

*   **Rack:** 3,000 pounds (about 1,360 kilograms)
    
*   **Power system:** Four ISS Solar Array Wings, each producing 31 kilowatts and weighing 2,500 pounds. That adds up to 10,000 pounds or 4,536 kilograms.
    
*   **Cooling:** Two ammonia-loop radiators from the ISS External Active Thermal Control System, with a combined weight of 3,300 pounds or 1,500 kilograms.
    
*   **Total mass:** 7,396 kilograms
    
*   **Launch cost:** 2,645 dollars per kilogram
    
*   **Total cost:** Just under $19.6 million
    

To be fair, the maths checks out if you accept the assumptions. But that is exactly the problem. The assumptions are tied to technology designed in the 1990s. None of these systems reflect what is available in 2025. And once you swap them out, the economics shift completely.

## **Power: Why 4,500 Kilograms is the Wrong Starting Point**

The ISS Solar Array Wings, or SAWs, are a common reference point in space power discussions. They are large, visible, and historically proven. But they are also outdated. Designed in the 1990s, these arrays used rigid panel structures, early gallium arsenide cells, and heavy deployment trusses. Their specific power is about **27 watts per kilogram**, or **12.4 watts per pound**. That might have been fine when launch cost was 50,000 dollars per kilogram, but it makes little sense today.

If you are building a power system in 2025, this is the wrong place to start.

### **Upgrade 1: iROSA, Already in Orbit**

Redwire’s iROSA system is a modern roll-out blanket array, now deployed on the ISS. It delivers far better power-to-weight performance. Each unit provides 20 kilowatts and weighs about 340 kilograms, which gives a specific power of around 58.8 watts per kilogram.

To power a 120 kilowatt system using iROSA, you would need about six units, which adds up to around 2,040 kilograms of solar arrays. That is already less than half the mass of the original SAW-based estimate.

### **Upgrade 2: Redwire’s Current-Generation Arrays**

According to Redwire’s datasheet, their newest roll-out arrays deliver 120 watts per kilogram. Using this, you would only need 1,000 kilograms of array mass to generate 120 kilowatts.

That brings the required solar mass down by over 3,500 kilograms compared to the legacy design. And this is not theoretical. These arrays are commercially available and being actively marketed.

### **Looking Ahead to 2030**

By 2030, solar technologies are expected to reach 200 watts per kilogram. This is driven by advances in thin-film photovoltaics, lighter structural materials, and the possibility of in-orbit assembly.

At that level of performance, you would only need 600 kilograms of solar array mass to power a 120 kilowatt compute rack. That is a reduction of 87 percent compared to the ISS-era baseline.

### **Why This Matters**

Reducing the power system mass from 4,500 kilograms to 1,000 or even 600 changes the economics completely. It does not just save money. It opens up the ability to co-launch with other systems, to scale deployments, and to rethink how orbital compute infrastructure is designed.

And this is before we look at cooling.

## **Cooling: Why 1,500 Kilograms is Also Too Much**

Once you have power, the next challenge is thermal rejection. A rack like the NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 draws about 120 kilowatts of power, and in space, almost all of that becomes waste heat. Unlike on Earth, you cannot rely on fans, airflow, or coolant loops venting into the atmosphere. In orbit, heat must be moved using fluid loops or heat pipes and then radiated away into space.

The original post referenced the ISS External Active Thermal Control System, which uses ammonia loop radiators. Each loop is quoted at 1,500 kilograms to reject 70 kilowatts. You would need two of them to manage 120 kilowatts, so the assumed radiator mass was 3,300 pounds, or roughly 1,500 kilograms.

Again, this system was built for a different mission. The ISS cooling setup was designed with full redundancy, large fixed radiators, and the ability to work across all orbital attitudes with a crew on board. None of that applies to a self-contained, purpose-built compute node.

### **Modern Options: Already More Efficient**

Thermal systems available in 2025 are significantly lighter. High-performance deployable radiators now achieve between 100 and 120 watts per kilogram. These are used on large satellites, space stations, and spacecraft, and are typically based on roll-out structures, loop heat pipes, or pumped two-phase systems.

If we take a conservative figure of 106 watts per kilogram, you would only need **1,132 kilograms** of radiator mass to reject 120 kilowatts of heat. That is already **400 kilograms lighter** than the ISS-based design.

### **Looking Ahead to 2030**

Future radiator systems are targeting **200 watts per kilogram**, especially for larger orbital platforms where mass and surface area efficiency become even more critical.

At 200 watts per kilogram, the required radiator mass drops to just **600 kilograms**. That is a saving of **900 kilograms** compared to the original estimate, and a **60 percent improvement** over today’s best available systems.

These newer systems are not just lighter. They are also more modular, easier to stow for launch, and faster to deploy once in orbit. They simplify thermal architecture, reduce mechanical complexity, and make it easier to scale up.

### **Why This Matters**

Cooling is often treated as secondary to power, but it carries the same mass penalties and integration challenges. Reducing radiator mass from 1,500 to 600 kilograms is a structural shift. It lowers launch cost, shrinks the deployed footprint, and simplifies both system design and mission planning.

And like with power, this change is possible using technology that already exists.

Now that we have updated the power and cooling systems, we can re-run the launch cost calculation with far more realistic numbers.

In the original post, the total system mass was estimated at **7,396 kilograms**. This included 4,536 kilograms of solar arrays, 1,500 kilograms of radiators, and a fixed 1,360 kilogram rack. At a launch cost of **2,645 dollars per kilogram**, the total came to **19.5 million dollars**.

Using current 2025-era hardware, we can now revise those numbers:

*   **Rack:** 1,360 kilograms (unchanged)
    
*   **Power (Redwire next-gen, 120 W/kg):** 1,000 kilograms
    
*   **Cooling (modern radiators, 106 W/kg):** 1,132 kilograms
    
*   **Total mass:** 3,492 kilograms
    
*   **Total cost at 2,645 dollars per kg:** 9.24 million dollars
    

That is a cost reduction of over 10 million dollars, without modifying the compute payload at all.

## **Looking Ahead to Lower Launch Prices**

So far, we have used a launch cost of 2,645 dollars per kilogram. This reflects the current rate for a dedicated Falcon 9 mission in 2025 and is the same figure used in the original post. However, this assumption may not remain valid for long.

SpaceX’s Starship is being developed specifically to reduce the cost of access to space. Although still in its early operational phase, many industry observers expect launch prices to fall to somewhere between 500 and 100 dollars per kilogram within the next several years. Elon Musk has mentioned a long-term goal of 10-20 dollars per kilogram, but that remains speculative and is not included here.

## **Summary: What Happens When You Stop Using 1990s Assumptions**

By updating just two subsystems, power and cooling, we cut total launch mass by more than half. The cost drops from 19.5 million dollars to 7 million using today’s launch pricing, and as low as 350,000 dollars if Starship reaches its long-term targets. Crucially, all of this is achieved without changing the compute rack.

This matters because orbital compute is no longer a fringe concept. Starcloud, Axiom, and China’s Star Compute constellation are already moving ahead with space-based data processing infrastructure. If we are going to compete, or even stay relevant, we need to stop designing systems as if we are still using ISS components from the 1990s.

The rack used in this analysis, the NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, is completely unsuitable for orbit. It is heavy, water-cooled, and built for a terrestrial data hall environment with air handling, steel framing, and floor mounting. It was never designed for vacuum, radiation, or robotic servicing.
