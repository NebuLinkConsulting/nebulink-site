As you are reading this, tens of thousands of satellites are streaking overhead. They are monitoring our crops, guiding our cars, and beaming calls from the most remote corners of the globe. But, as their numbers swell, a looming threat remains overhead. If even a 1cm spec of debris collides with a spacecraft, it can make the million dollar machine inoperable. Worse than that, if the collision is severe enough, there is a risk that the impacted satellite might shatter into thousands of additional pieces of debris, each of which could collide with further satellites in a runaway loop that could render space unusable for generations.

# The relationship between satellites in orbit and collision avoidance

This is a known issue within the industry, but I am writing it today after reading [Hugh Lewis](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hugh-lewis-31242117/)’ [analysis of Starlink’s latest semi-annual report.](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/starlink-manoeuvre-update-july-2025-hugh-lewis-utkhe/?trackingId=N87tkJD8esU01XWVzjUgEw%3D%3D) This analysis outlined that 144,000 avoidance burns were executed within six months, nearly double the previous rate. Lewis’ analysis reveal an alarming trend: as Starlink’s population climbs, the yearly collision-avoidance manoeuvre rate explodes. Figure 1, taken from the analysis, plots the annual burns against the average number of active satellites, fitting an exponential curve.

It’s a harsh signal that our ground-based, human-in-the-loop processes are rapidly hitting their limits. Even accounting for Starlink tightening it’s probability threshold, this curve shows that each additional thousand satellites adds disproportionately more burns, rapidly overwhelming ground-based operations.

# Starlink can act as a proxy for the industry as a whole

You might be wondering, “Why just Starlink?”. Well, they’re the biggest operator who issues semi-annual reports that provide a rare, consistent multi-year insight into how avoidance burns stack up as satellites multiply. Most other operators do no put this level of detail into the open, so Starlink’s numbers are our best proxy for the true operational burden we face up here.

As satellite fleets and debris clouds multiply, our current collision-avoidance methods will buckle under the load. Relying on ground stations and human-in-the-loop decisions introduces delays, uncertainty, and cost at every turn. Here are the biggest headaches we face today:

1.  **Latency in Detection and Response**
    
2.  **Ballooning Alert Volumes**
    
3.  **Rapidly Growing Uncertainty**
    
4.  **Fragmented Tracking Data**
    
5.  **Coordination Overhead**
    
6.  **Fuel and Lifetime Trade-Offs**
    
7.  **High Relative Velocities**
    

To think in more general terms, if the problem you’re trying to solve is growing exponentially, any solution that scales linearly, or worse, relies on humans, will quickly collapse under its own weight. We need a collision-avoidance method that scales at least as fast as the threat: one that pushes sensing, decision-making, and maneuver execution onboard each spacecraft, rather than shuttling data back and forth to Earth. This shift from ground-centric to distributed, autonomous operations is the only way to keep pace with the coming tidal wave of satellites and debris. Whether this is a literal movement of computing onto the satellites themselves, onto data centres in orbit, or even done autonomously on a satellite-by-satellite basis is up to better minds than myself.

# This is a problem we’ve solved before

At first glance, this sounds like a really hard problem, right? Imagine trying to track tens of thousands of satellites, each zooming along at thousands of miles an hour, against a backdrop of millions of debris fragments, and doing it all without a single centralized beacon or traffic controller. Yet every day, some 12,000–14,000 aircraft crisscross our skies with near-perfect safety records, despite being built by different manufacturers, flown by crews speaking different languages, and guided by many different air-traffic systems.

How do the planes not crash into each other? The secret is an on-board, peer-to-peer system fitted onto nearly every commercial plane. This system continuously sends and listens for specific radio signals from nearby aircraft. By timing how long these signals take to return, the system can build a live, 3D map of surrounding traffic. If another plane gets too close, it’ll warn the pilot, and if the pilot doesn’t resolve it, it will issue a message to tell the pilot of one plane to climb and the other plane to descend, without the need for ground control to be involved, using a computer, an antenna, and a cockpit display.

As someone with an interest in air crash investigation, it’s well known that early planes were much more dangerous. The need for a TCAS-like system was first seen in the mid-20th century after the 1956 Grand Canyon mid-air collision when two perfect condition passenger aircraft crashed into each other, resulting in the creation of the FAA.

In the following decades, further collisions revealed that “see‑and‑avoid” procedures and intermittent radar coverage were inadequate. Investigations repeatedly found that neither pilots nor controllers had reliable, real‑time awareness of nearby traffic. In response, the FAA mandated what we now know as the TCAS for virtually all commercial aircraft, embedding peer‑to‑peer autonomy as a non‑negotiable safety layer in every cockpit.

# **So why does space not have TCAS**

Even though orbit is becoming as congested as our skies, a TCAS‑style solution for satellites runs into three major roadblocks:

1.  **No universal beacon**. Satellites rely on a patchwork of ground radar stations, optical telescopes and proprietary links, each with its own quirks of accuracy, delay and access. Without a common “space transponder”, they simply cannot share real-time positions.
    
2.  **Harsh orbital conditions**. Debris and satellites whip by at over 10 km/s, and safe manoeuvres must be planned hours or even days ahead. Fitting the precise sensors and computing power needed to predict these encounters into every small LEO craft strains weight, power and thermal limits.
    
3.  **Fragmented rule making**. Aviation had ICAO and the FAA to hammer out global standards; in space, dozens of national agencies, military bodies and commercial operators each guard their own orbit data and procedures. Agreeing on a single protocol and trusting autonomous systems to fire thrusters without human sign‑off will take time, co‑ordination and probably a few more close calls.
    

# But I think it is possible

However, nearly every satellite already carries a basic telecommand-and-telemetry antenna. By piggy‑backing a lightweight beacon onto that existing link, sending only position and status, neighbouring spacecraft could listen in and plan avoidance. They would pick up each other’s beacons, build a live, peer-to-peer traffic map and decide onboard whether to manoeuvre.

Modern satellites now pack surprisingly powerful, energy‑efficient computers and tiny radar or laser sensors into a small package, all drawing only a small amount of power. By ramping up computing only when needed and using simple passive cooling, these components can run real‑time calculations and spot objects under a metre away without adding significant weight or complexity. As a result, even the smallest satellites can support an onboard system that detects nearby craft or debris and makes manoeuvre decisions autonomously.

Another issue is that space, unlike airspace, is not owned or controlled by any single nation, so there is no ICAO‑style authority to mandate a common beacon. Yet almost every major satellite programme depends in some way on US or EU government funding, licences or launch services. By making the carriage of a standard “Space Traffic Transponder” a condition of export approvals, launch contracts or grant awards, much as the FAA tied Mode C/S transponders to aircraft certification, regulators could force universal adoption. Over time, this approach would copy aviation’s top‑down mandate in orbit, even without a single global space authority.

The final challenge is standardisation. Despite the many ‘standards’ in the industry, if you ever speak with a RF engineer they’ll tell you it’s more myth than reality. I naively assumed that something like ‘CCSDS compliant’ would mean they followed the standards, but a much smarter RF engineer than me explained that even the manufacturer’s data sheets contradict themselves and it’s much harder to be compatible than it seems.

**A Grave Warning for Orbit**

In aviation, regulations were written in blood, shaped by the sacrifices of pilots and passengers who died before systems like TCAS became mandatory. In space, without people aboard to galvanise action, catastrophic debris cascades won’t command the same urgency. If we do not adopt that hard‑won, life‑or‑death mindset and make onboard collision avoidance a compulsory standard, we risk condemning the orbital commons to an environmental crisis from which it may never recover.
